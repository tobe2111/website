Clinic blog and website content is found more often and read to the end when it starts from the questions patients actually ask, rather than what the clinic wants to say. The basic process is to collect questions from consultations, phone calls and chat, write a short answer first, and run a medical-advertising check and a physician review before posting.

This article describes general content practice and is not legal advice. For whether specific wording is permitted, check with a medical advertising review body or a legal professional.

Key takeaways

  • Good topics already exist inside your clinic. Questions that repeat in consultations are search queries.
  • A short answer in the first paragraph helps patients, search engines and AI search understand the page quickly.
  • Guaranteed results, comparisons with other clinics and patient testimonials are flagged under Korean medical law.
  • Medical content should always be checked by a physician before it goes live.

Where do you find topics?

Your staff are the best source. Ask your consultation manager, front desk and nurses to log the questions they hear several times a day for one week. You will see questions such as "When can I exercise after treatment?", "How long does the pain last?" and "How many visits will I need?". Group similar ones and write about the most frequent first, and you get content that has search demand and also saves consultation time.

How should a post be structured?

Write the title as a question in the words patients search with. Put the conclusion in two or three sentences in the first paragraph, and say that results vary from person to person. Then explain why, and what the treatment steps are, in plain language. Finish with possible discomfort or side effects and when a visit is needed, leaving nothing out.

What does medical advertising law require you to watch?

A clinic blog post can count as advertising. Wording that promises outcomes, such as "guaranteed results" or "no side effects", claims that you are better than other clinics or treatments, and using patient reviews in a way that misleads about effectiveness are typical problem areas. Before-and-after photos also need care because they can mislead. We cover this in more detail in medical advertising rules to check before your clinic runs an ad.

Checklist before posting

  • Is the title phrased the way patients actually ask?
  • Is the answer in the first paragraph, with a note that results vary?
  • Is there no wording that guarantees results or compares you with other clinics?
  • Are side effects, cautions and when to visit included?
  • Has a physician checked the medical content?
  • Are photos free of patient data or anything that identifies a patient?

How do you keep it going?

One or two posts a week, ticking questions off your list, lasts longer than writing a lot at once. Staff can also send the link straight to patients, which improves how inquiries are handled.
