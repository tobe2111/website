US B2B buyers look at whether your product is good, and also at whether you are "a company we can start trading with right now". Have your English materials, export price list, regulatory compliance and logistics terms ready in advance, so the first meeting leads to sample requests and a contract.

Key takeaways

  • An English company profile, a catalog and a price list with trade terms are the basics.
  • For food and cosmetics, check US FDA registration and English labeling requirements first.
  • Work backward from the US shelf price to set a supply price you can negotiate with.
  • Having someone handle work on the ground in the US makes everything after the contract much faster.

What do buyers look at in the first meeting?

Buyers want to confirm four things: whether the price leaves room for profit on their shelves, whether you can deliver on time and consistently, whether you comply with US regulations, and whether you have a plan to support sales once the product is in store. Materials that answer these questions directly matter more than product descriptions.

Which materials should you prepare?

ItemWhat it should include
English company profile and catalogBrand story, hero products, sales track record in Korea
Export price listTrade terms (such as FOB), wholesale price, suggested retail price, minimum order quantity (MOQ)
Product specification sheetIngredients in English, size, shelf life, packaging dimensions
Regulatory documentsFDA registration suited to your product category, English label review
BarcodesUPC barcodes, widely used in US retail
Production and logisticsProduction lead time, monthly supply capacity, sample shipping schedule

Food comes with requirements such as facility registration and nutrition labeling, and cosmetics now carry facility registration and product listing obligations. Requirements differ by product category, so check them before you export. Some retailers also require product liability insurance.

How should you design your pricing?

US distribution passes through importers, distributors and retailers, and each step adds its own margin. If you start from your Korean supply price, the shelf price often ends up far higher than competing products. Research what similar products sell for in US stores first, then work backward from that price to set your supply price.
