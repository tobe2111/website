Before you ship cosmetics or food from Korea to the US, first confirm how your product is classified in the US. Obligations such as facility registration, product listing, labeling and notice before shipment differ by classification, and a product that is a cosmetic in Korea is sometimes treated as a drug in the US. Fixing things after shipment costs far more, so it is safer to check before you quote.

What follows is general guidance. For how the rules apply to you, check with the relevant authorities or a professional. Details can change, so before you proceed, also read the latest guidance from each agency, starting with the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

Key takeaways

  • Most cosmetics and food fall under the FDA. The system is not one of approval before sale. The business carries responsibility for registration, labeling and safety.
  • Some products, such as sunscreen, are cosmetics in Korea but are classified as over-the-counter drugs in the US.
  • For food, the basics are facility registration, appointing a US agent and prior notice for every shipment.
  • Labels are in plain view, so problems with them surface easily. Get English labeling, the full ingredient list and allergen labeling right first.

How is your product classified in the US?

The US divides products not only by ingredients but also by what they claim to do. A product that cleanses or beautifies the skin is a cosmetic, but one that says it treats or prevents a condition, or affects a function of the body, is regarded as a drug. This means the same cream can be classified differently depending on the wording on its label and product page. The FDA judges intended use by looking not only at the label but also at advertising, online promotional copy and what consumers expect of the product. Expressions such as hair growth, cellulite reduction, regulating melanin production and cell regeneration are examples that read as drug claims. US law has no in-between category called "cosmeceutical".

What do cosmetics need?

The US widened the obligations of cosmetics businesses with the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA). The framework has four parts.

  • Facility registration: facilities that manufacture or process cosmetics to be sold in the US register with the FDA and renew on a set cycle. Foreign facilities also appoint an agent in the US.
  • Product listing: the responsible person submits information, including ingredients, to the FDA for each product marketed.
  • Responsible person: the manufacturer, packer or distributor whose name appears on the label is the responsible person. It keeps records that support safety, and when a serious adverse event is reported to it, it reports to the FDA within the set deadline.
  • Label: state in English the identity of the product, net contents, the full ingredient list in descending order of content, the name and address of the business, and any required warnings. If you leave wording in another language, such as Korean, on the label, the required information must also be given in that language.

There is a provision exempting small businesses from facility registration and product listing. However, there is a separate threshold for the size of business that qualifies, and some products are excluded from the exemption, such as products that touch the mucous membrane of the eye, injected products, and products intended to alter appearance for more than a day, so you need to check whether it applies to you. Product listing is not submitted once and finished. Changes are updated every year. A product that is both a cosmetic and a drug must meet the requirements of both, and there are exceptions to cosmetics registration for products and facilities subject to drug regulations, so check separately which route applies. Cosmetic ingredients are not approved before sale, with the one exception of color additives. For color cosmetics, it is a good idea to check at the formulation stage that the color additives are permitted ones.

The FDA does not issue certificates or documents confirming facility registration. A "registration certificate" handed over by an agency is not a document issued by the FDA.

What does food need before shipment?

The FDA does not approve or certify imported foods, importers, labels or individual shipments in advance. The system is that food can be brought in if the facilities that handle it are registered and the shipment is notified in advance. In return, goods arriving at the port are subject to inspection and are detained if they do not meet US requirements.

  1. Food facility registration: facilities that manufacture, process, pack or hold food to be consumed in the US register with the FDA and renew on a set cycle. Foreign facilities appoint a US agent.
  2. Be ready for the importer's verification: US importers are obliged to verify that their foreign suppliers produce food in line with US food safety standards. This is why buyers ask for hygiene management certificates and process documentation.
  3. Extra steps for certain products: for acidified foods and low-acid canned foods, meaning some foods that are sealed and stored for a long time at room temperature, such as canned goods and retort pouches, the manufacturing process is filed with the FDA separately from facility registration.
  4. Label: state in English the Nutrition Facts panel in the US format, ingredients, major allergens, net contents and business information. The major food allergens defined by US law are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame. Sesame and soybeans are common in Korean sauces and snacks, so check the ingredients one by one.
  5. Prior notice: for every shipment, notify the FDA electronically before the goods arrive in the US. It can be filed through the customs entry system or through the FDA's dedicated prior notice system, and food sent by international mail is covered too. The deadline for notice can differ by mode of transport, so align it with your logistics provider in advance. If you miss it, the goods can be held at the port.

Is meeting federal rules enough?

No. Individual states have their own rules. A widely known example is California's warning label system (Proposition 65), known as a system that requires a warning statement when a product contains a substance listed by the state. On top of this come the test reports and documents that large retailers and online marketplaces require on their own.

So when you talk with a buyer, it is a good idea to ask first in which states and through which channels the product will be sold. That sets the scope of the documents to prepare. What buyers check at the quoting stage is covered in what to prepare before meeting US B2B buyers.

Common mistakes

  • Adding only an English sticker to the Korean label. Required items and units differ, so in some cases the label has to be made again.
  • Translating Korean functional claims as they are. Expressions permitted in Korea, such as wrinkle improvement or whitening, can read as drug claims in the US.
  • Presenting registration as approval. Facility registration or product listing does not mean the FDA has approved the facility or product. The FDA does not approve cosmetics and food before sale, so advertising "FDA approved" is a false statement.
  • Missing minor ingredients. A meat extract in a soup base or sauce can change which agency has jurisdiction and whether the item can be exported.
  • Assuming samples are fine. Samples sent to trade shows or buyers also go through customs. For food, samples for testing the market and samples for product development are in principle subject to prior notice too. If it is in the form of food that can be eaten, it is not exempt simply because the quantity is small.
