If you are new to selling online in Japan, the lower-burden order is usually to test demand on Qoo10 Japan first, then expand to Rakuten once you know which products sell and your operations are in place. Qoo10 is relatively easy for Korean sellers to enter. Rakuten takes more fixed cost and more operating effort, but it reaches a broad customer base. The order can change depending on your product and the customers you are aiming for.

Key takeaways

  • Before the platform, check which rules apply to your product category in Japan.
  • Qoo10 runs on discount events and Rakuten on building up your own shop, so the preparation differs.
  • Do not open both at once. Refine your Japanese product pages and customer service on one platform first.
  • Fees and listing terms can change, so check each platform's latest guidance before you proceed.

The costs also take different shapes. Rakuten is known to combine a fixed fee that depends on the plan with usage fees in proportion to sales, and sellers also fund part of the points given to customers as a set percentage of sales. Check the fee table first to see how much goes out even in a month with no sales. Qoo10 is known to work mainly by taking a commission when a sale happens. Its flagship discount events can come with participation conditions, so ask in advance whether you can join right after you open.

Rakuten's strength is its points. Rakuten points can be earned and spent across telecom, travel and payment services as well as the shopping mall, so shops often run campaigns that give two or three times the usual points.

These two are not the only options. There are also platforms such as Amazon Japan, where competition happens product by product. One difference is that Amazon has many customers who arrive already knowing what they want to buy, while Rakuten has many who browse across categories. The test is the same wherever you sell. Are the people who would buy your product there, and can your team handle the way that platform operates?

What should you check before the platform?

Product regulations. There are broadly two ways to bring products into Japan and sell them, and the requirements depend on which one you use.

  • Shipping directly from Korea to the consumer: when an order comes in, you send it from Korea by international delivery. It is easy to start, but it is treated as a personal import by the consumer. Personal imports of cosmetics and quasi-drugs are allowed without a license only for the buyer's own use and within set quantities. In other words, one person ordering a lot of the same product can go beyond that limit.
  • Importing into Japan and selling from local stock: delivery is fast and returns are easy to accept. In exchange, importing and selling cosmetics requires a Japanese cosmetics manufacturing and marketing license. If your buyer does not hold this license, the options are to obtain it or to hand the import to an agent that already holds one. Japanese labels have to be in place at the point of sale, not at customs clearance, so the importer often applies them after clearance. Food requires an import notification and a check against ingredient standards.

You also need to settle first whether the product is a cosmetic or a quasi-drug. Japan separates the two by the effects the product claims. Quasi-drugs sit between cosmetics and pharmaceuticals, in a position similar to "functional cosmetics" in Korea. They have a separate approval process, so preparation takes much longer. Shampoo, toothpaste and hair dye can also fall into this category depending on their ingredients. To sell a product marketed as a functional cosmetic in Korea as an ordinary cosmetic in Japan, removing the claims from the label is not enough. The formulation itself has to be acceptable as a cosmetic.

Advertising claims are part of the rules too. Japan defines in a list the range of effects a cosmetic may claim, so translating a Korean product page as it is can cause problems. This is general information. Confirm how it applies to your case with the relevant authorities or a professional.

How do Japanese customers choose products?

In general, online shoppers in Japan are known to read information carefully before buying. So more of the preparation goes into information than into the product itself.

  • Product page: list size, volume, ingredients, directions and precautions without gaps. Detailed product information is also a way to reduce returns. Awkward machine-translated sentences cost trust by themselves, so have a native speaker review the text.
  • Shipping information: state exactly when the order ships and roughly when it arrives. If you ship from Korea, do not hide that fact or the number of days it takes.
  • Inquiries: answer politely in Japanese and within a set time. Late or wrong answers end up in reviews as they are.
  • Reviews: until the first few build up, ads rarely lead to purchases. Products tend to have fewer reviews each than in markets such as the US, so a few good reviews carry a lot of weight.
  • Payment: quite a few customers choose to pay when the goods arrive. Check which payment methods the platform supports.

In what order should you start?

  1. Settle the product rules and the sales model. Whether you ship direct or hold local stock changes everything that follows.
  2. Cut the range to three to five products. If you list everything, the quality of translation and customer service drops. Start with the products that have many repeat purchases in Korea.
  3. Create Japanese product pages and reply templates. Write out the likely questions and answers in advance.
  4. Open on one platform and join an event. Sales and reviews during the event show which products work.
  5. Expand based on the numbers. Once some products sell repeatedly, consider local stock and listing on other platforms.

If you are weighing online sales against wholesale, the criteria in Exporting to China: B2B wholesale or cross-border e-commerce first? apply to Japan as well. For listing and translation costs, check whether the Export Voucher program, a Korean government support program, can cover part of them.

Common mistakes

  • Translating the Korean product page as it is. The Korean format of one long image with text built in is hard to translate, hard to edit and hard to search.
  • Selling only during events and leaving the shop idle the rest of the time. When customers gained in an event come back, stock and customer service have to be ready.
  • Competing on discount rate alone. After fees, international shipping and return costs, the price easily leaves nothing. Calculate what you keep per unit first.
  • Leaving tax and shipping out of the price. If the amount changes at checkout, drop-offs and inquiries go up. Show the total the customer pays from the start.
  • Not planning for returns. If you have not decided where returned goods are received and how they are handled, the loss on each case grows.
