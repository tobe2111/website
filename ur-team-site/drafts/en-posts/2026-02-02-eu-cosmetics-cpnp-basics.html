To sell cosmetics in Europe, you appoint a Responsible Person (RP) with an address inside the EU, and that Responsible Person notifies the product to CPNP (the Cosmetic Products Notification Portal). CPNP is not an approval procedure. It is a notification made before sale. The time and cost go not into the notification itself but into the safety documentation that has to be in place before it.

What follows is general guidance. For how the rules apply to you, check with the relevant authorities or a professional. Ingredient rules and procedures can change, so also read the latest guidance before you proceed.

Key takeaways

  • Without a Responsible Person, a cosmetic cannot be placed on the EU market. The Responsible Person's name and address also go on the label.
  • CPNP notification is not a review or approval by the authorities. The phrase "CPNP certified" is not accurate.
  • The ingredient review, the safety assessment report and the product information file must be ready before notification.
  • The UK runs a system separate from the EU, so prepare for it separately.

What is CPNP, and how is it different from approval?

CPNP is an online notification portal run by the European Commission, and it is free to use. You upload the product category, Responsible Person details, images of the label and packaging, formulation information and so on. The information is viewed in two places. The authorities of each member state use it for market surveillance and consumer information, and poison centers designated by member states check the formulation for treatment purposes when an incident occurs.

The important difference is the order. The authorities do not review documents and then permit sale. The business meets the requirements on its own, then notifies and sells. Verification happens after sale. If you cannot produce the documentation when the authorities ask for it, that is when the problem arises. Each member state's authorities are responsible for checking products on their market. Once you notify CPNP, you do not need to notify again separately in each member state.

Who is the Responsible Person (RP), and what do they do?

The Responsible Person is a legal entity or individual inside the EU that is responsible for the product's compliance with the EU Cosmetics Regulation. The role is taken by the manufacturer, the importer, or a third party they designate. For a product made in Korea, there are two routes: the importer bringing it into the EU takes the role, or you separately designate another entity in the EU or a specialist service provider. Who the Responsible Person is must be recorded in a document, not agreed verbally. A product with no Responsible Person cannot be placed on the EU market.

Who you appoint as Responsible Person is also tied to your sales strategy. If you make one buyer the Responsible Person, that company's address is printed on the label and the documentation builds up with them. This can become an obstacle later when you trade with buyers in other countries or change partners. If you plan to work with several buyers, consider an independent Responsible Person. When using a service provider, ask in advance about the scope of what they take on and how the cost changes as the number of products grows.

What do you need to prepare before notification?

  1. Ingredient review: check the full ingredient list against the EU's list of prohibited substances and its list of substances with use limits. Colorants, preservatives and UV filters can be used only if the EU has explicitly permitted them. You can look up restrictions by ingredient in the European Commission's ingredient database (CosIng), but that database is for reference and the legal standard is the annexes to the Cosmetics Regulation. If an ingredient that was fine in Korea is caught here, the formulation has to change, so do this first.
  2. Safety assessment report: a document in which a qualified safety assessor reviews the safety of the product and signs it. Collecting raw material data and test reports from the manufacturer takes the most time.
  3. Product information file (PIF): brings together the product description, the safety assessment report, the manufacturing method and a statement of compliance with good manufacturing practice, and the evidence supporting claimed effects.
  4. Label: state the Responsible Person's name and address, net contents, the date of minimum durability or the period after opening, precautions, the batch number, the function of the product and the full ingredient list. Member states can require label information in their own language, so the label needs another look as the number of countries you sell in grows. Also confirm with the Responsible Person the country-of-origin marking that applies to imported products. The CE mark seen on electronics is not a mark for cosmetics, and putting it on is in fact a violation.
  5. CPNP notification: the Responsible Person uploads it once the preparation above is done. The notification also states whether the product contains nanomaterials. For products containing nanomaterials other than colorants, preservatives and UV filters, there is a separate procedure, apart from the general notification, for notifying in advance several months before sale. An ingredient in nano form is written in the ingredient list with "nano" in brackets after its name.

Does the same notification work for the UK?

No. CPNP is a portal for products placed on the EU market, and the UK has run its own system since leaving the EU. So for the UK it is usual to arrange a Responsible Person in the UK and a UK notification separately. Even within the UK, some products in Northern Ireland are subject to rules different from those in Great Britain (England, Scotland and Wales), so you need to check by where you will sell. The framework of the documentation required is fairly similar to the EU's, so companies often prepare on the basis of what they made for the EU. Check the detailed requirements in the latest guidance from the UK authorities, and if the UK is part of your European plan, ask about both at the quoting stage.

Common mistakes

  • Advertising notification as if it were certification. Notification does not mean the authorities have confirmed the product is safe.
  • Carrying over Korean functional claims as they are. Claims need supporting evidence, and wording that suggests treatment goes beyond the scope of a cosmetic. For products on the border between cosmetic and medicine, member state authorities decide product by product.
  • Changing the formulation and leaving the documentation as it is. When ingredients or concentrations change, the safety assessment and the notification must be updated too.
  • Treating products with different shades and scents as one. If formulations differ, confirm with the Responsible Person whether each must be notified.
  • Taking the contract with the Responsible Person lightly. Write down in advance who keeps the documentation when the contract ends and how the label will be changed.
