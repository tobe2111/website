Exports to the GCC (the council of six countries: Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and Oman) start with two decisions. How far can your product meet halal requirements (halal means permitted under Islamic law)? And on what terms will you grant sales rights to a local distributor? The first decides whether you can enter. The second decides the shape of your business for years after you enter.

This post is general information. Confirm how it applies to your case with the relevant authorities or a professional. Detailed requirements in each country can change, so check each authority's latest guidance before you proceed.

Key takeaways

  • The GCC is spoken of as one market, but product registration and customs clearance are handled separately in each country.
  • Whether halal certification is mandatory depends on the product and the country, and the certifying body must be one the importing country recognizes.
  • Logistics, certification and distribution costs stack up, so competing on low price is hard. Prepare the grounds for a higher price point.
  • A distributor agreement is harder to end than to sign. Write down scope, term, minimum purchase and the registration holder from the start.

What kind of market is the GCC?

The six countries rely on imports for a large share of their consumer goods, and retail revolves around large supermarket chains and shopping malls. There is a framework for aligning standards and customs systems, but product registration and label review are carried out separately by each country's authorities. Completing registration in one country may not carry over to the country next door.

So begin by choosing one first country. Priority goes to a country where you have a distributor candidate and where the registration procedure for your product is clear. Building registration and a sales record there and then expanding to neighboring countries takes less money and time than opening all six at once.

Is halal certification required?

It depends on the product. For some products, such as meat and processed meat products, certification is a condition for customs clearance. For others it is not mandatory, but distributors and consumers choose by the certification mark. Which case applies has to be checked after you settle the product and the importing country.

Taking Saudi Arabia as an example, meat and poultry need a halal certificate for every shipment to clear customs, and that certificate must be issued by a body recognized by the Halal Center of the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA). Imports of alcoholic beverages are prohibited, as are pork and food ingredients containing pig-derived fat, rennet (the enzyme used to set cheese) or gelatin.

If you decide to get certified, choose the certifying body first. The certification is only effective locally when it comes from a body recognized by the authorities of the importing country. Even if you already hold a certification, check again whether it is accepted in your target country. Apart from halal, you also need to work through the requirements for Arabic labels, product registration and remaining shelf life.

Labels follow the common GCC standard for packaged food. In Saudi Arabia, the label must be in Arabic or include an Arabic translation, and it carries the product name, country of origin, ingredients listed in descending order of quantity, and the expiry date. The country of origin must be marked in a way that cannot be easily removed. Package design is also subject to checks. Avoid images of pigs, religious phrases and photos of people that do not suit local sensibilities.

Why enter at a premium price point?

The cost structure makes it so. Long-distance freight, storage and transport suited to a hot climate, certification and registration costs, Arabic labeling work and the margins of the importer and the distributor are added one after another. With all of that in the price, it is hard to compete head-on with low-priced products coming in from nearby countries.

So products that can explain why they cost more have the advantage. Prepare three things.

  • Proof: put the facts that support the price, such as ingredients, manufacturing method and test results, into materials in English and Arabic.
  • Packaging: have sets designed for the seasons when gift demand is high, and an appearance that stands out on store shelves.
  • Price discipline: set the recommended retail price the distributor will keep to, and a way to manage the volume released online.

Ramadan (the Islamic month of fasting) and the holiday that follows are a period when spending is concentrated and working hours are shorter. The dates change every year, so check that year's dates and plan your shipping schedule backward from them.

What should a distributor agreement settle?

In the GCC, entering through a local distributor or agent is the usual route, and some countries have a system that protects registered local agents. The United Arab Emirates is the leading example. The most common approach is to appoint a distributor or agent for the whole country or for specific emirates. When the agency agreement is registered with the authorities, it is protected by a commercial agency regime that is designed in favor of the local agent. A registered agreement comes with exclusive import rights, and termination requires either mutual consent or approval from the authorities. Once signed, an exclusive agreement can be hard to end even when results do not follow. If a dispute arises, the Arabic translation of the contract takes precedence over the English version, so the translation needs review too. Write the following into the contract.

  1. Territory and channels: limit them to the countries and distribution channels the distributor's sales really reach, not all six countries.
  2. Term and minimum purchase: give exclusivity a fixed term, and make it conditional on meeting an annual minimum purchase quantity.
  3. Registration holder: decide in whose name the product registration and trademark are held, and how they are handed over when the contract ends. Some companies set up a local office to keep control of product registration and leave only sales to the agent.
  4. Marketing costs: split who pays for listing fees, promotions and samples, and how much.
  5. Termination and stock: write down the grounds for termination, the notice period and how remaining stock is handled.

Agency laws differ by country and continue to be amended, so before you sign, have the agreement reviewed by a professional who knows the current law of that country. For unit price terms, see Export quote basics: how EXW, FOB, CIF and DDP differ.

Common mistakes in exporting to the GCC

  • Granting exclusivity for all six countries to the first buyer met at a trade show.
  • Checking only the main ingredients and missing the source of minor ingredients such as flavorings or capsules.
  • Getting certified by a body that is not recognized in the target country.
  • Not calculating the time production and transport take, so that too little shelf life remains on arrival.
  • Assuming that once registration is done in one country, the other countries follow automatically.
