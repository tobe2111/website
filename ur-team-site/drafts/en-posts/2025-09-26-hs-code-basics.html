The HS code is the number that determines, all at once, how much duty your product pays in the country you are exporting to, which certifications and inspections it needs, and whether it can benefit from an FTA (free trade agreement). That is why you check it before quoting or looking for buyers. If the number is wrong, the unit prices and schedules built on it go wrong with it.

Key takeaways

  • The first 6 digits of an HS code are the same worldwide. The digits after that differ by country.
  • Duty rates, import requirements and FTA rules of origin all follow this number.
  • Duty is assessed on the classification made by customs in the importing country, not the exporting country.
  • If classification is unclear, consult a customs broker and use the Korea Customs Service's advance ruling system.

What is an HS code?

HS is the international system for classifying goods, created by the World Customs Organization (WCO). It assigns numbers to traded goods so that customs in any country places the same item in the same position. The first 6 digits are shared by the countries that have joined the convention, and each country subdivides further after that as it needs. The system is used not only to assess duties but also to compile trade statistics. It is revised every few years, so a number can change after a revision even if the product stays the same.

Numbers are assigned by material, use and degree of processing, not by product name. Even among products called "tea", dried tea leaves, a powder to mix with water and a ready-to-drink beverage can be placed in different positions. So a number cannot be decided from the product name alone.

Korea uses its own number with extra digits added after the first 6, commonly called HSK. Other countries have their own number of digits and their own subdivisions. The US uses 10 digits, and even for the same item it has one schedule for export declarations (Schedule B) and another for import declarations (HTS), so the last 4 digits can differ. This means the number used in Korea cannot simply be copied onto the importing country's documents. Only the first 6 digits are common.

For example, if USD 10,000 worth of goods falls between a number with a 0% duty rate and one with 8%, the duty the buyer pays is either USD 0 or USD 800. The buyer will try to recover that difference in price negotiations. These are made-up numbers for illustration.

FTAs start from the same number. Each agreement sets rules of origin by product, which answer the question "How much of the product must be made in Korea for it to count as Korean?". The HS code is the key for finding those rules. If you get the number wrong, you prepare documents to the wrong rule.

How do you confirm the HS code for your product?

  1. Gather materials that describe the product. Raw materials and their proportions, use, processing method and packaging form are the basis for classification.
  2. Find candidate numbers. Use the classification search provided by the Korea Customs Service to see how similar goods have been classified.
  3. If there are two or more candidates, consult a customs broker. Sets, products made of several materials and products with several functions are especially likely to be classified differently.
  4. For items you will keep exporting, get an official answer. If you apply to the Customs Valuation and Classification Institute of the Korea Customs Service for an advance classification ruling, it decides the number and replies.
  5. Confirm the importing country's number separately. Ask the buyer or a local customs agent for the number used in that country, the rate and the import requirements.

An advance ruling can be requested not only by the company exporting or importing but also by the manufacturer of the export goods and by a customs broker. You apply through UNI-PASS, the Korea Customs Service's electronic clearance system, and the rule is to send a sample together with a product description. Goods that need composition analysis carry an analysis fee. When you declare goods identical to those in the reply, customs applies the classification as notified. If you do not accept the result, you can request one re-examination within a set period.

Details such as rates, import requirements and ruling procedures can change, so check each agency's latest guidance before you proceed.

What if the exporting and importing countries use different numbers?

This is not unusual. Customs in two countries can place the same item under different headings. Since it is the importing country's customs that assesses duty, calculate duty and import requirements on the importing country's classification. For the export declaration made in Korea, use the Korean number.

The issue is documents that both countries look at, such as an FTA certificate of origin. Agree with the buyer before shipment on which number the documents will be aligned to. For a large transaction, another option is to ask the buyer to use the importing country's advance confirmation system. The US makes the binding rulings its customs authority has issued on the classification of imported goods public and searchable, so you can look up in advance how similar goods were classified. If the number you received through an advance ruling in Korea differs from the importing country's classification, you can report it to the HS international dispute desk of the Customs Valuation and Classification Institute. Common mistakes with HS codes

  • Picking the number with the lower duty. A number is not chosen. It is determined by the facts of the product. A wrong declaration can mean paying the tax again later.
  • Using the number of another company that sells a similar product. A small difference in composition or use changes the classification.
  • Copying the full Korean number onto the importing country's documents. The later digits differ by country.
  • Changing the product and not looking at the number again. When raw materials or composition change, check the classification again.
  • Using a number written down by the carrier without checking. Responsibility for the declaration comes back to the owner of the goods.
