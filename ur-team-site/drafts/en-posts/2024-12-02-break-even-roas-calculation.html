Break-even ROAS is the selling price divided by the money left from one sale before ad costs are paid. A business that keeps 40% of the selling price breaks even at a ROAS of 250%. A business that keeps 20% breaks even at 500%. That is why the answer to "Is a ROAS of 300% good enough?" is different for every business.

Key takeaways

  • ROAS (return on ad spend) shows revenue only. To judge whether you made money, you need to know your margin.
  • Break-even ROAS = selling price ÷ money left before ad costs × 100.
  • The lower the margin, the more steeply break-even ROAS rises.
  • Compare revenue and ad spend only after lining up how VAT, cancellations and fees are treated.

Why is there no money left when ROAS is high?

ROAS shows how much revenue each unit of ad spend produced. If KRW 100,000 of ad spend (amounts in this post are in Korean won) brought in KRW 300,000 of revenue, ROAS is 300%. The ad console of Naver, Korea's largest search portal, shows it under a label that translates as "ad return rate" and calculates it by dividing revenue from ads by total cost. The problem is that the KRW 300,000 is not all yours. Product cost, shipping, packaging and payment fees come out first.

If KRW 90,000 is left from KRW 300,000 of revenue after those costs, then after paying KRW 100,000 for ads you are down KRW 10,000. This is why the screen says 300% while the bank balance shrinks. For the basic meaning of the metrics, you can start with our guide to reading CTR, CVR and ROAS.

How do you calculate break-even ROAS?

The calculation below is a made-up example to show the logic.

  1. Write down every cost of selling one unit. Say a product sells for KRW 40,000, with a product cost of KRW 16,000, shipping and packaging of KRW 4,000, and payment and sales fees of KRW 4,000.
  2. Subtract them from the selling price. KRW 40,000 − KRW 24,000 = KRW 16,000. This is the money left before ad costs.
  3. Work out the margin. KRW 16,000 ÷ KRW 40,000 = 40%.
  4. Divide the selling price by the money left. KRW 40,000 ÷ KRW 16,000 × 100 = 250%. This is your break-even ROAS.

Check the math. If KRW 1,000,000 of ad spend brings in KRW 2,500,000 of revenue, the money left is 40% of KRW 2,500,000, which is KRW 1,000,000. That equals the ad spend exactly, so profit is zero. If the same ad spend brings in KRW 4,000,000, KRW 1,600,000 is left, and KRW 600,000 is profit after paying for ads.

40%250%You must sell 2.5 times your ad spend to break even
50%200%You must sell 2 times your ad spend to break even

As the table shows, when the margin halves, break-even ROAS doubles. For a thin-margin product, it is hard to get past break-even however much you refine the ad operation. In that case, the first step is not advertising. It is raising the amount bought per order with bundles, or changing the shipping cost structure.

This break-even point does not include fixed costs such as rent and labor. Break-even ROAS is the line at which you at least do not lose money because of ads. For the business as a whole to make money, the target has to be higher.

You set a target ROAS by deciding how much room to add on top of break-even. For example, for a product that breaks even at 250%, you need 500% if you want to keep a profit equal to your ad spend. When revenue is 5 times ad spend, the money left is 2 times ad spend.

The same calculation gives you the value to enter when you use a feature that adjusts bids automatically toward a target return, such as Target ROAS in Google Ads. If you enter a value below break-even, the platform will faithfully grow revenue that loses money.

Can a business that takes bookings use the same formula?

Yes. Use one customer's payment in place of the product. For example, if the average payment is KRW 80,000 and KRW 16,000 goes to materials and card fees, KRW 64,000 is left. KRW 80,000 ÷ KRW 64,000 × 100 = 125% is break-even.

Two things need to be looked at together, though. If the person who performs the treatment or teaches the class is paid per session, include that in the costs. Also, the ROAS on the ad screen can only be calculated when a revenue value is recorded for each conversion (a person who saw the ad taking a target action such as a purchase or booking). An online store can be set up to pass the payment amount automatically, but for a business that takes bookings by phone or consultation, this value is often empty. In that case, calculate revenue yourself by multiplying the number of inquiries by the booking rate and the average payment. On platforms such as Google Ads, where you can assign the same value to every conversion, entering the value of one inquiry worked out this way makes the number on the screen usable for decisions too.

In businesses where many customers come back, there is a reason to keep ads running even if the first visit falls a little short of break-even. Make that call after actually counting your repeat visit rate.

What people often leave out of the calculation

  • Mixing VAT bases. If revenue includes VAT and ad spend excludes it, ROAS comes out higher than it really is. On Naver Ads, bids are entered excluding VAT, but Naver states that total cost and return on ad spend in reports are shown including VAT. Each platform displays this differently, so put both on the same basis.
  • Not subtracting cancellations and returns. Revenue on the ad screen is often counted at the time of order. Recalculate with the revenue that was actually settled.
  • Comparing numbers with different counting windows. The ad screen credits ads not only with purchases right after a click but also with purchases some days later. Naver Ads shows these separately as direct and indirect conversions, and how long the latter are counted depends on the settings. Before comparing with last month, check that the settings are the same.
  • Leaving agency fees and production costs out of ad spend. All the money spent in order to advertise goes into the denominator.
  • Forgetting discount coupons. A coupon lowers the selling price, and the margin goes down with it.
  • Looking only at the overall average. If margins differ by product, break-even differs too. Start by calculating your top-selling products separately.

The rules ad platforms use to count revenue can change. Check each platform's latest guidance before you start.
