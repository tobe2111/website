A landing page is the page people arrive on after they click an ad. Its first screen has to say three things before anyone scrolls: what is sold here, why the visitor should do it here, and what to press now. Visitors who cannot find these three leave without reading the rest.

The five seconds in the title is not a measured figure. Read it as a way of saying that visitors judge a page in a very short time.

Key takeaways

  • The job of the first screen is not to persuade. It is to confirm "I am in the right place".
  • The headline states what the visitor gets, not who the company is.
  • The button asks for one action only.
  • Check the page on a phone, not on the large monitor on your desk.

What three things must the first screen include?

Visitors arrive with three questions in mind the moment they click an ad. Each element of the first screen answers one of them.

What to conveyWhat the visitor is thinkingElement that does the job
What, and for whomIs this what I was looking for?Headline
Why hereHow is this different from the others?Subheading and one line of proof
Next actionSo what do I press?One button

The three come in order. People who do not know what the page offers will not read the reason, and people without a reason will not press the button. So place the headline, the proof and the button from top to bottom, in the order the eye travels.

Everything else has to give up its place on the first screen: company history, a long greeting, a carousel of banners that rotate automatically. If there are three banners, visitors see only the first one, and whatever the other two say never reaches them.

How should you write the headline?

There are two rules. The first is to use the same words as the ad. If someone searches for "partial remodeling quote", clicks an ad and does not see those words on the first screen, they feel they have landed in the wrong place. Match the key words of your ad copy and your headline. This does not mean copying the search term letter for letter. It is enough that the visitor can tell that what they were looking for is right here.

This rule does not come from visitors alone. Search ad platforms also evaluate the page an ad leads to. The Quality Score in Google search ads is made up of three components: expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience. Landing page experience means how relevant and useful the page is to the people who click the ad. Each of the three is shown as above average, average or below average, compared with other advertisers whose ads appeared for the same search. To improve this component, Google recommends showing people what they were looking for right on the page, carrying the benefit and the call to action from the ad through to the page, making the page easy to get around on a phone, and making it load quickly.

Keep two points separate. This score is a diagnostic tool that tells you where to fix things. It is not a performance metric to be raised for its own sake. Also, the rate at which inquiries or purchases happen does not affect the landing page evaluation. A better rating does not mean more inquiries, so count inquiries separately.

The second rule is to write what the visitor gets, not your own story. Here is an example.

  • Before: An interior design company that grows together with its customers
  • After: Partial remodeling for apartments in the 30-pyeong range (pyeong is the Korean unit of floor area). We measure on site and send you a written quote first

The first sentence would make sense on any company's page. The second says who the service is for and what happens first. Under the headline, add one line of proof. Write only what can be confirmed as fact, such as qualifications, years in operation or the terms of the service. Decorative wording is not proof.

Choose photos by the same standard. A photo of your actual store, product or work in progress tells visitors what you sell faster than a stock photo of a smiling model.

How many buttons should there be?

One highlighted button works well. When a consultation request, a phone call, KakaoTalk (the messaging app most people in Korea use), sign-up and a download sit side by side at the same size, visitors try to choose and then give up. Decide on the one action you want most from this page and make it stand out. Keep the others small.

Work on the button text too. "OK" or "Submit" does not say what happens when you press it. Write the action and the benefit together, as in "Get a free quote" or "Pick a consultation time". A line right under the button that lowers the barrier, such as "Just leave your phone number", reduces hesitation. The actual form then has to be that short. The screen after the button is an extension of the promise the first screen made. If someone presses "Get a free quote" and is asked to create an account first, that promise is broken. What changes on a phone?

Pages are built on large monitors, but the people who click ads are often holding a phone. The first screen on a phone is much narrower, so the same page runs into different problems.

  • One large photo fills the screen and pushes the headline and button below the fold.
  • A pop-up that opens on arrival covers the first screen.
  • Images with text built into them shrink until the text cannot be read.
  • A heavy video makes the first screen slow to appear.

Fixing the button to the bottom of the screen so that it follows the visitor is one option. If it is large enough to cover the content, though, it gets in the way.

Checking is simple. Turn off Wi-Fi and click your own ad on a phone. The time you wait for the screen to appear and the area you see without scrolling are exactly what your visitors experience.

First screen checklist

  1. If you show the first screen briefly to someone who does not know your business and then hide it, can they say what is sold there?
  2. Are the key words of the ad copy in the headline?
  3. Is the headline a sentence that would not make sense on another company's page?
  4. Is the line of proof something that can be confirmed as fact?
  5. Is there one highlighted button, and does its text say what the visitor gets by pressing it?
  6. On a phone, are the headline and the button both visible without scrolling?
  7. Is nothing above covered by a pop-up or an automatic banner?

For item 1, ask a family member or a friend. The person who built the page already knows the answer and cannot check it alone.
