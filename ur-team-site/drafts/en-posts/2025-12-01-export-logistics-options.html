If orders are few and the product is light, start with direct shipping, sending each order from Korea. Once orders from one country come in steadily, move to holding stock locally. Local stock splits into two routes: running a warehouse yourself, or using a 3PL (a logistics company that handles storage, packing and dispatch for you). At the start, the 3PL route is usually the lighter burden. The basis for the decision is not delivery speed. It is the total cost per order and the stock that gets tied up.

Key takeaways

  • Direct shipping carries no stock risk, but freight per order is high and delivery is slow.
  • Local stock delivers fast, but you pay freight, duties and storage before anything is sold.
  • A warehouse you run yourself only fits large volumes. A 3PL charges for what you use, so it is light to start.
  • To send stock into a country, you need a party that acts as the importer there.

Fulfillment run by an online marketplace itself (a service covering everything from receiving to delivery) is also a form of 3PL. It is convenient within that marketplace, but you need to check separately whether it can also handle orders from other sales channels.

When choosing a 3PL, look at whether you can see stock and dispatch records on screen right away, as closely as you look at the fee schedule. Stock that is far away can only be checked through numbers.

How long does direct shipping work?

The advantage of direct shipping is that you send only what has sold. It fits the stage when you do not yet know which product will sell in which country. For light products with a reasonable unit price, the freight per order is also easier to absorb.

It is time to move when you see these signs.

  • Complaints and cancellations increase because of delivery time.
  • The price with freight added becomes noticeably higher than competing local products.
  • The cost of sending a return back to Korea exceeds the price of the product.

Tax is another variable. The de minimis threshold, below which goods are not charged duties and taxes, differs widely by country, and some countries, such as the US, have suspended this exemption. Where the exemption does not apply, the recipient has to pay tax even on a small amount. Unexpected tax leads to refused deliveries. So work out the landed cost by country in advance, meaning the product price plus freight, duties and local taxes, which is the total the customer will actually pay, and show it on the sales page. Decide who pays return freight at the same time. Terms under which the seller ships with taxes paid are explained in the DDP section of our Incoterms post. Details such as exemption thresholds and clearance procedures can change, so check the latest guidance from each country's authorities and your carrier before you proceed.

What changes when you hold stock locally?

Delivery gets faster and freight per order goes down, because you ship a lot at once. In return, three new tasks appear.

The first is import clearance. There has to be an importer who takes responsibility for bringing the goods in locally. If you have no local entity, a buyer, a local partner or an agency service often takes this role. Depending on the product, you also need certification and local-language labels.

The second is tied-up stock. Your unsold goods are sitting in someone else's warehouse in another country, so choosing a counterpart you can trust comes first, and you should also check insurance that covers incidents in transit and in storage. Storage fees keep running even if nothing sells. For food and cosmetics with a shelf life, disposal costs can arise as well. Freight to bring goods back once they are sent costs about as much as sending them.

The last is management. You have to check stock quantities, inbound and outbound records and the status of returns from a distance. So at the start, send only a few well-selling items in small quantities. For countries such as China, where the route splits between general trade and cross-border e-commerce, also see our post on B2B wholesale versus cross-border e-commerce for China.

How do you compare costs?

For each method, add up the money it takes for one order to reach the customer's door.

  1. Direct shipping: international freight per order + packaging + taxes borne by the seller
  2. Local stock: (bulk freight + duties + clearance costs) ÷ quantity sent + storage + dispatch fee per order + local parcel delivery
  3. Both: the cost of handling returns, and the cost of stock that will remain unsold

For example, say direct shipping costs KRW 15,000 in freight per order (amounts are in Korean won). If freight and clearance for sending 500 units at once come to KRW 1,500,000, that is KRW 3,000 per unit. If storage, dispatch fees and local parcel delivery add another KRW 6,000 per unit, the total is KRW 9,000, a saving of KRW 6,000 per order. But if 100 of the 500 units do not sell, the cost of those 100 units and their storage fees eat into the freight you saved. These are made-up numbers for illustration, and actual amounts should be confirmed with quotes from a freight forwarder and a logistics company. A forwarder can tell you at the quoting stage not only the freight but also port charges, documentation fees, insurance premiums and its own fees, and it handles clearance in the importing country by connecting with a local customs broker. If you leave these costs out, your cost per unit is understated.

Checklist before choosing a logistics method

  • How many orders come in steadily from that country each month?
  • How much is freight per order relative to the product's weight, volume and selling price?
  • Is it a product that cannot be held in stock for long because of shelf life or season?
  • Who takes the importer role locally?
  • Have you met import requirements such as certification and labels?
  • Have you decided where returned goods will be received and how they will be handled?
  • Does the 3PL quote list all of the receiving, storage, dispatch and return fees?
