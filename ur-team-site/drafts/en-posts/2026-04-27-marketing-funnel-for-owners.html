A marketing funnel is a picture of the path a customer takes, from first hearing about your business to buying and coming back, divided into stages. What an owner needs to know is not the terminology. It is one thing: the stage where the most customers drop out. Once you find that stage, you know where to spend your money and time.

Key takeaways

  • The funnel runs from awareness to interest, inquiry or visit, purchase and repeat purchase.
  • If you choose just one number for each stage and write it down, you can see where the funnel leaks.
  • If it leaks in several places, fix the lower stages, closer to the purchase, first.
  • If you increase ads while the bottom is leaking, you only increase the number of customers who drop out.

What is a marketing funnel?

A funnel is wide at the top and narrow at the bottom. Not everyone who sees an ad comes to your store, and not everyone who comes buys, so the number of people shrinks at each stage. The funnel is a drawing of that shrinking shape.

The shrinking itself is not a problem. Every business has it. What to look for is the one place where the number falls unusually sharply. That is the place your business should work on first.

How do you draw your own funnel?

Draw five boxes on a sheet of paper. In each box, write what the customer does and one number you can count. The table below is an example based on a local store. For an online shop, replace visits with product page views and inquiries with add-to-cart.

StageWhat the customer doesNumber to countWhat you mainly work on
AwarenessSees the store name in search results or an adImpressionsAds, blog, local communities
InterestClicks and looks through the store informationClicks, views of your Naver Place listing (the business listing on Naver, the Korean search portal)Main photo, title, introduction text
Inquiry or visitCalls, books or comes inNumber of calls, bookings and direction requestsReviews, price information, booking button
PurchasePaysNumber of payments, average spend per customerCustomer service, consultation flow, menu structure
Repeat purchaseComes back or recommends you to othersNumber of returning customers, number of reviewsFollow-up contact after the visit, benefits for regulars

You do not need exact numbers from the start. Call logs are enough for the number of calls, and marks in your booking ledger are enough for return visits. Counting the same way every month comes before accuracy.

If you have a website or an online shop, an analytics tool can draw this picture for you. In Google Analytics, funnel exploration shows how many people moved on and how many dropped out at each step, once you define the steps yourself. Someone who skips a step in the order you defined is counted as dropping out there. You can choose whether to look only at people who entered at the first step (a closed funnel) or to include people who entered at a later step (an open funnel). If many of your customers click an ad and land directly on a product page, the open funnel is closer to what really happens.

How do you find the stage that leaks?

Divide the number for each stage by the number for the stage above it. Say that over one month your ad was shown 10,000 times, 300 people clicked, 15 made an inquiry and 3 paid.

  • Impressions to clicks: 300 out of 10,000, or 3 in 100
  • Clicks to inquiries: 15 out of 300, or 5 in 100
  • Inquiries to payments: 3 out of 15, or 1 in 5

The number that ad dashboards call the conversion rate corresponds to "clicks to inquiries" here. Naver Ads defines the conversion rate as conversions divided by ad clicks. This number is only captured when a tracking script is installed on your site, and inquiries that end outside the site, such as phone calls and visits, are easily missed. So for the inquiry and payment stages, look at the numbers you counted yourself alongside it.

These ratios alone do not tell you which stage is bad, because the normal range differs by industry. So compare them with two things. One is your own numbers from last month. The other is what customers said. Think back to why the 12 people who inquired did not pay, and reasons come out: they hung up after hearing the price, there was no available date, or the reply was late. The numbers tell you where, and the customers' words tell you why.

Why fix the lower stages first?

Because the lower stages cost less to fix, and the effect reaches every customer coming in from above. In the example, if payments rise from 3 to 6 out of 15 inquiries, revenue doubles without spending anything more on ads, assuming the amount per payment stays the same. If you only double ad impressions, ad spend doubles and so does the number of customers who drop out.

Fixing the lower stages is usually nothing grand. It means not missing inquiry calls, telling people the price and the time required up front, and making the booking button visible right away on the page where people land after clicking. We listed the reasons customers leave the landing page in Landing pages that leak ad spend: seven common mistakes.

In order, it looks like this.

  1. First check that you are not losing customers at the purchase and repeat purchase stages.
  2. Clear the obstacles at the inquiry or visit stage (reviews, price information, how to book).
  3. After that, work on the photos and copy that bring more clicks.
  4. Last, widen the top with ads and content.

Common mistakes when reading a funnel

  • Looking only at the top. Impressions and clicks show up right away on the ad dashboard, so people look only at those. Inquiries and payments have to be counted by the business itself.
  • Fixing every stage at once. You can no longer tell what worked. Change one stage a month.
  • Leaving repeat purchases outside the funnel. Getting a past customer to come back often costs less than bringing in one new customer.
  • Different people counting each stage by different standards. If the conversions on the ad dashboard and the bookings at the store do not match, decide first which one is your reference. On an ad dashboard, both completed purchases and add-to-cart actions can be recorded as conversion types, so also check what was counted as one conversion.
