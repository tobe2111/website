There is no fixed starting amount for an ad budget. First work out how much money one customer leaves you. Then decide how much of that you will spend to bring in a new customer, and multiply by your target number of customers. That is your budget. If the amount is too small, though, you never collect enough numbers to tell what is working, so spend enough to be able to judge within a month.

Key takeaways

  • A budget starts from what one customer leaves you, not from revenue.
  • Set the ceiling first: the most you can spend to bring in one customer.
  • Your first budget does not buy profit. It buys the data you need to make decisions.
  • Production costs and agency fees belong in the budget along with ad spend.

If you are new to advertising, a sensible order is to start with a test budget, get real numbers, and then work back from a target using those numbers. The percentage-of-revenue method is easy to calculate, but it cannot answer the question "why that percentage?".

How do you calculate the budget for your own business?

The calculation below is a made-up example to show the logic. The amounts are in Korean won (KRW). Replace them with your own numbers.

  1. Work out what one customer leaves you. Say the average payment is KRW 50,000, and KRW 20,000 is left after variable costs such as materials.
  2. Decide how much of that goes to ads. You decide to spend up to half, KRW 10,000, to bring in one new customer. This amount is your ceiling.
  3. Assume a rate at which clicks become customers. If you assume that 3 out of 100 ad clicks lead to a booking, you can spend up to KRW 300 per click.
  4. Multiply by your target. If the goal is 30 new customers a month, that is 30 × KRW 10,000 = KRW 300,000. In clicks, that is 1,000.
  5. After a month, replace the assumptions with real numbers. If it turned out to be 2 bookings per 100 clicks, each customer cost KRW 15,000. That is over the ceiling, so you fix the keywords or the landing page, or adjust the target.

In businesses where many customers come back, the ceiling in step 2 can be set higher. The basis is then the money a customer leaves over several visits, not on the first visit alone. For how to read click-through and conversion rates, see our guide to reading CTR, CVR and ROAS.

What is the minimum you need to spend to be able to judge?

Think in terms of how much data builds up, not in terms of money. With only three or four clicks a day, a month can pass and you still cannot tell which keyword brought in customers. You cannot know whether a difference of one or two inquiries is chance or skill.

For example, in a business where a click costs about KRW 300, spending KRW 10,000 a day builds up about 1,000 clicks a month. In a business where a click costs KRW 3,000, the same money buys 100 clicks a month. In the second case, you need to cut down to three or four keywords so that each keyword collects enough numbers to judge.

So ask the question in reverse: "How much do I need per day to collect enough clicks within a month to see differences between keywords?" In businesses with a high cost per click, the same amount of data costs more. If the budget is short, reduce the scope before you reduce the amount. Use fewer keywords, narrow the area, and concentrate on one channel.

How should you split the budget?

The smaller the budget, the more you concentrate it in one place. Start with ads shown to people who are already searching for your type of business, and add other channels once the numbers are confirmed. When you do run several channels, keep most of the budget where results are proven and set aside only a part for testing new channels. With a separate test share, a failed experiment does not shake the whole plan.

Apart from ad spend, keep some money for improving photos and the landing page. If you buy clicks and the page they land on is weak, the budget simply leaks away. We list what to check in seven common landing page mistakes that waste ad spend.

A daily budget means different things on different platforms

Once you have a monthly budget, you enter it in the ad console as a daily amount. In Google Ads, the daily budget is an average. On days with heavy search traffic it spends more than the amount you set, and on quiet days it spends less. The monthly total is kept within your daily budget multiplied by the average number of days in a month. There is no need to be alarmed if one day's spend is higher than your setting. Look at it by the month.

On Naver Search Ads, the ad platform of Korea's largest search portal, the daily budget is a safeguard against overspending. You set it for each campaign, and ads stop automatically when the amount is reached. Naver also notes that stopping takes time, so slightly more than the set amount can be spent. If your budget runs out in the morning, you can turn on even distribution, which spreads delivery across the day. This can leave you short of exposure in your busiest hours, so first check at what times inquiries come in.

Minimum bids, product lineups and budget features on ad platforms can change. Check each platform's latest guidance before you start.

Common mistakes when setting a budget

  • Counting only ad spend as the budget. If photo, video and page production costs and agency fees are left out, actual spending goes over the plan.
  • Copying the amount the business next door spends. If average order value and profit structure differ, the same amount means something different.
  • Turning ads off after a week. Daily results go up and down. Set the date for your decision in advance and wait until then.
  • Running ads only when business is good. If you turn ads off in a slow month, next month's customers drop as well.
  • Not writing down where inquiries came from. Simply asking "How did you hear about us?" on the phone gives you a basis for the next budget.
