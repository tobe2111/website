Getting ready to receive international patients starts before advertising, with whether interpreting, payment and aftercare connect without a break. The places where patients feel anxious are not inside the consultation room. They are the reception desk where nobody understands them, a bill that differs from what they expected, and having nowhere to ask once they are back home. When a person in charge and the documents are fixed for these three points, a hospital can receive its first patient.

The content below is general information. Confirm how it applies to your case with the relevant authorities or a professional.

Key takeaways

  • Interpreting is needed at every step from reception to payment, not only in the consultation room.
  • Most payment disputes arise because the amount and conditions were not given in writing before the visit.
  • Agree the contact channel and the reply hours for after the patient returns home before they leave the hospital.
  • In Korea, only registered medical institutions may attract foreign patients. This is the first line of preparation.

How far should interpreting be prepared?

Medical interpreting is different from everyday interpreting. Explanations and consent have to be conveyed in full, and the hospital has to be able to confirm that they were. If interpreting is left to an acquaintance or a guide the patient brought along, the hospital has no way of knowing when content is cut or changed.

  • People. Have staff who can do medical interpreting, or set up a system for calling in outside interpreters by appointment. A test system that certifies medical interpreting ability has been established by government notice, so when you look for interpreters you can use whether they passed it as one criterion. Taking bookings from international patients only on the days and hours when interpreting is available is another option.
  • Documents. Prepare professionally translated versions of consent forms, precautions and cost information. Documents translated on the spot with a translation app can hardly serve as a basis for consent.
  • Step-by-step guidance. For phrases that are repeated, such as reception, waiting, payment and the next appointment, make guidance cards in the patient's language. The front desk keeps running even when the interpreter is away.

If there is only one interpreter, that person's day off becomes the hospital's gap. Decide in advance how to cover it.

If there are costs charged separately, such as an interpreting fee or a document fee, list them as items on the quote. A cost first heard at the payment counter leads to distrust even when the amount is small. The principle for explaining prices is the same as for domestic patients. The price list the hospital has posted and the amount it quotes must match.

How do you manage care after the patient goes home?

Of the three, this is the part most often left empty. Once back home, the patient cannot come in again when a question comes up. So send them out of the hospital with the following in hand.

  1. One contact channel. Choose a messenger commonly used in the patient's country and use the hospital's official account. If messages go to a staff member's personal account, the records disappear when that person leaves.
  2. Reply hours. There is a time difference, so write it down: "We reply on weekdays between these hours, Korean time."
  3. Translated precautions. Give them both on paper and as a file.
  4. What to do if a problem arises. Tell the patient in advance what to send to whom, and how the hospital will respond.
  5. English documents. Explain how to have treatment-related documents issued that can be shown to a medical institution at home.

Ask for a review after this process is finished. A review written by a patient whose questions were answered in time after returning home reads differently from one written while waiting for an answer.

What should you check for registration and paperwork?

To attract foreign patients in Korea, a medical institution must meet the requirements and register with its city or provincial government. Applications are made online in the Foreign Patient Attraction Information System.

  • Requirements for medical institutions. There must be a specialist for each department that will receive foreign patients, and the institution must be enrolled in medical malpractice liability insurance or a medical liability mutual aid association. Check whether your current insurance covers foreign patients.
  • Requirements for facilitators. Companies that refer patients register separately, with surety insurance, capital above a set level and an office in Korea. Whether a company is registered can be looked up in the same system.
  • Obligations after registration. The registration has a validity period and must be renewed before it ends. The registration certificate must be displayed, patients must be able to find out about matters concerning their rights and interests, and excessive commissions may not be demanded. There is a ceiling on attraction commissions, set by government notice.
  • Performance report. Each year, the previous year's results are reported to the city or provincial government with jurisdiction. Along with items such as nationality and department, the report states the route by which each patient came, for example through a facilitator's referral or on their own. Collect this information from the moment of reception so you do not have to hunt for it all at once at the start of the year. Failing to report can lead to a corrective order or cancellation of registration.

Paying or receiving commissions with an unregistered referrer can become a problem, so check the other party's registration before you work together. There is also a separate system under which registered medical institutions are evaluated on their services for foreign patients and their patient safety systems, and receive accreditation. Detailed standards can change, so check the latest guidance from the relevant authorities before you proceed. Checklist before receiving your first patient

  • Have you completed registration for attracting foreign patients, and checked the registration of your partner companies?
  • Are the days and hours when interpreting is available stated in the booking information?
  • Do you have translated versions of the consent forms, precautions and cost information?
  • Does the quote template sent before the visit state the conditions that may change the amount and any separate costs?
  • Have you actually tested overseas cards and mobile payments?
  • Are the deposit and cancellation policy ready in the patient's language?
  • Are the official account, the person in charge and the reply hours fixed for contact after the patient returns home?
  • Does every member of staff know whom to call when an international patient walks in without a booking?
