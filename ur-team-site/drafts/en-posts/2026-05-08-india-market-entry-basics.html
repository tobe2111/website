If you are considering India, check two things before the size of the market. Can your product obtain the certification or registration India requires? And is the local consumer price, after duties, taxes and distribution margins are all added, at a level that will sell in that market? If either one is blocked, the deal stops just before the contract even when you have found a buyer.

This post is general information. Confirm how it applies to your case with the relevant authorities or a professional such as a licensed customs broker. Detailed requirements can change, so check each authority's latest guidance before you proceed.

Key takeaways

  • In India, each product group has its own authority and its own certification or registration system. Start by confirming where your product belongs.
  • Packaged consumer goods must carry set declarations, including the MRP (maximum retail price).
  • Do not build the price up from your ex-factory price in Korea. Work backward from the local consumer price.
  • Certifications and registrations often carry the name of the local importer or agent, so the choice of your first partner has long-lasting effects.

Why check certification first when exporting to India?

Because certification is a condition for customs clearance. Marketing and price can be adjusted along the way, but cargo sent without a required certification stops at the port. Storage charges build up, and getting the goods back also costs money.

Lead time is another variable. With document review, test reports and in some cases a factory audit, it can take considerable time for a certification to be issued. That is why the certification schedule comes before any delivery date you promise a buyer.

Even within one product group, whether a product is covered depends on the specific item. So the first step is to fix the HS code (the product classification number). Only once the code is settled can you check the duty rate and whether certification applies.

BIS certification is voluntary in principle, but products that the Indian government has designated through Quality Control Orders can be imported only with the standard mark and a valid certification. When an overseas manufacturer applies, it must appoint an agent who resides in India, and samples drawn during the factory audit are tested only at laboratories recognized by BIS. A test report obtained in Korea cannot be used in its place, so build sample shipping and the audit schedule into your timeline.

Declarations are written in English or Hindi. Food carrying only a label made for the Korean domestic market will not clear customs. In addition to the ingredient list, country of origin and expiry date, it must show whether it is vegetarian or non-vegetarian with the prescribed symbol. There is also a condition that a certain proportion of the shelf life must remain at the time of clearance, so calculate the shipping schedule and the shelf life together.

How do you calculate backward from the local price?

Packaged consumer goods in India have the MRP printed on them. It is the ceiling above which retailers cannot sell, and it includes taxes, transport and the commissions of each distribution stage. Since the price the consumer sees is fixed on the package, the calculation is more accurate when it starts there.

  1. Research the MRP of the products you will compete with locally and decide the price range your product will sit in.
  2. Subtract the retailer's margin and the distributor's margin from the MRP. This is the price at which the importer sells to the distributor.
  3. Subtract the importer's margin and local logistics and marketing costs.
  4. Subtract duties and taxes. Besides the basic customs duty, other taxes are charged at the import stage, so a calculation based on one duty rate easily differs from the real burden. Confirm with a customs broker what applies to your product and how much.
  5. Subtract international freight and insurance. What remains is the export unit price you can receive.

If the unit price you arrive at does not cover your cost and your margin, you need to change the pack size, simplify the packaging or reset the price range. Korea and India have a Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA), and for some products a lower agreement rate applies when you have a certificate of origin. Confirm with a customs broker whether your product is covered and what the rules of origin are. What should you look at when choosing a local partner?

In India, the importer is often not just a purchaser but the party to the certification or registration. If a registration is in the importer's name, you may have to register again when you change partners later. The declarations printed on the package also include the importer's name and address, so a change of partner means remaking the packaging as well. Before signing, put in writing whose name the registration is in and how it will be handed over when the contract ends.

It is also safer to start with a narrow scope. In India, language, consumer habits and distribution structure differ from state to state. If a partner asks for nationwide exclusivity, ask which regions and channels its sales really reach, and consider granting exclusivity for a fixed period, tied to that scope and to a minimum purchase quantity.

Common mistakes in entering India

  • Sending samples and a quote first, without checking certification, because a buyer showed interest.
  • Estimating the local price by adding only freight to the ex-factory price in Korea. Taxes and two or three layers of distribution margin are missing.
  • Shipping in the packaging used in Korea. If declarations are missing, sticker work has to be redone locally.
  • Granting exclusivity before settling the registration holder and the termination terms.
  • Treating India as one single market and not deciding on a region and a channel.
