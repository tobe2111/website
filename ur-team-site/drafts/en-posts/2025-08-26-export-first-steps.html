If you are preparing to export from Korea for the first time, the right order is to settle three things first, "which country, which product, at what price", and look for buyers after that. If you meet buyers first, you often get stuck on certification and pricing and end up back at the start. Prepare in this order: market choice, regulatory check, export pricing, introduction materials, buyer sourcing, samples and contract, then logistics.

Key takeaways

  • Start a first export narrow: one country, one or two products.
  • Check certification and labeling rules before you meet buyers. This is the step that takes the longest.
  • Calculate the export price separately, adding packaging, transport, customs clearance and local margins in turn to your domestic supply price.
  • You do not have to do it all alone. Bring in a customs broker, a freight company and support programs at each step.

In what order do you prepare for export?

  1. Choose the country and product. Choose where you can sell, not where you would like to sell.
  2. Check regulations and certification. Find out whether this product can enter that country and what you need to have in place.
  3. Build an export price list. Set unit prices by trade term and a minimum order quantity.
  4. Prepare introduction materials. Make a company profile, a product catalog and specification sheets in the local language or in English.
  5. Find buyers. Go through trade shows, export consultation events, online trading platforms and local partners.
  6. Send samples and sign a contract. Confirm quantity, unit price, payment method and lead time in writing.
  7. Ship the goods and collect payment. A freight company and a customs broker handle shipment and clearance.

If you skip the first three steps, you stall at step five. The first things a buyer asks are "Do you have that certification?" and "How much is it on these terms?".

How do you choose the country and product?

You may want to knock on doors in several countries at once, but each country needs its own labels, certifications and price list. Narrow it down to one country and one or two products. There are three criteria.

  • Can it get in? Are the ingredients or raw materials permitted in that country, and can you handle the certification required?
  • Does the price work? After adding freight and duties, does it still compare with similar local products?
  • Does the shelf life hold? After subtracting transport and clearance time, is enough shelf life left?

At this stage, check the HS code first. The HS code is a number that classifies goods moving between countries, and duty rates and import requirements are set by it. The first six digits are shared across countries and the digits after that are assigned by each country, so the number used in Korea and the number used in the importing country can differ in the final digits. The same product can carry a different rate depending on its classification, so it is safest to have a customs broker confirm it. The number is not only for looking up duty rates. It is written on shipping documents such as the commercial invoice and the certificate of origin, it is the basis for judging whether you qualify for lower rates under a free trade agreement, and it is what you search by when you research a market using trade statistics by country.

If the classification of your product is unclear, you can use the Korea Customs Service's advance classification ruling. Before an export or import declaration is filed, the exporter or importer, the manufacturer of the export goods, or a customs broker submits a product description and a sample, and the Customs Valuation and Classification Institute decides the classification number and replies. Applications are filed through UNI-PASS, Korea's electronic customs clearance system. For goods where a sample is hard to submit, a written explanation and photographs can take its place, and for food products you also submit materials such as a raw material composition table and a manufacturing process chart. The reply takes time, so it is a good idea to apply before you meet buyers. Note that this answer is the judgment of Korean customs. It does not mean customs in the importing country will use the same number, so confirm the local classification separately with the buyer or a local customs agent.

When should you check certification and regulations?

Before you look for buyers. Depending on the country, products such as food, cosmetics, electrical goods and baby products require advance registration, certification or local-language labels. Preparation can take months in some cases, so if you start after talks with a buyer have gone well, the opportunity cools in the meantime.

What to check comes down to four things: whether the product is allowed to be imported, whether there is registration or certification to obtain in advance, what must be written on the label and in what language, and whether the importer needs any qualification. Requirements differ by country and product and can change. This post is general guidance. For how the rules apply to you, check with the relevant authorities or a professional. In some markets, even within the same country, requirements differ depending on whether you enter through general trade or through cross-border e-commerce, so decide your entry route as well.

How is an export price different from a domestic price?

An export price is not your domestic supply price converted at the exchange rate. Costs are added layer by layer until the goods are on a shelf in the local store.

What is added to the priceDetailsWhere to find out in advance
Export packaging and labelsLocal-language labels, export cartons, palletizingYour own calculation
Inland transport and export clearanceTransport from the factory to the port or airport, and declaration costsFreight company, customs broker
International freight and insuranceSea or air freight, cargo insurance premiumFreight company
Local duties and taxesImport duty and local taxes such as VATCustoms broker, buyer
Local distribution marginsThe profit added by the importer, the wholesaler and the retailerBuyer, local partner

Subtract these layers backward from the local consumer price and you get the unit price you can receive. If that unit price leaves no profit, you need to choose a different country or product. How many of the layers you bear is set by the trade terms. For details, see our post on export quotes and the Incoterms EXW, FOB, CIF and DDP.

Checklist before you start

  • Have you narrowed the target country and product down to one or two?
  • Have you confirmed the HS code and that country's import requirements with a customs broker?
  • Do you have a trade business code? This is a number given to businesses engaged in trade for the purpose of export and import statistics. Sole proprietors and corporations with a Korean business registration certificate apply to the Korea International Trade Association (KITA) online or in person. KITA member companies are issued one without a separate application.
  • Do you have a price list showing unit prices by trade term, minimum order quantity and lead time?
  • Do you have a company profile and product specification sheets in English or the local language?
  • Have you set aside stock for samples and decided your principle on sample costs?
  • Have you set a baseline for payment terms, such as the share paid in advance?
  • Have you looked for support programs you can use, such as Korea's Export Voucher program?
