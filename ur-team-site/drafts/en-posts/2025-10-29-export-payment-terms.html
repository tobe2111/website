With T/T, the buyer sends payment directly by bank transfer. With an L/C (letter of credit), the buyer's bank promises to pay if the documents meet the stated conditions. T/T is simple and cheap, but depending on when the money is sent, one side carries the risk. An L/C reduces risk by bringing a bank in, at the cost of higher fees and a heavier document burden.

Key takeaways

  • With T/T, payment in advance favors the seller and payment afterward favors the buyer. That is why split payment is widely used.
  • Under an L/C, the bank pays on the documents, not the goods. If the documents differ from the conditions, payment can be delayed.
  • The newer the relationship, the larger the amount and the less familiar the country, the more you should start from safe terms and relax them as trade builds up.
  • Set payment terms together with Incoterms and lead time so your cash plan adds up.

How do T/T and L/C differ?

T/T (telegraphic transfer) is the buyer sending money from their bank to the seller's account. The process is simple and fees are low. The issue is timing. If the full amount is received before the goods are made (payment in advance), the buyer carries the risk of not receiving the goods. If it is sent after the goods are received (payment afterward), the seller carries the risk of not being paid. So payment is often split: part at contract, and the rest before shipment or in exchange for the shipping documents.

An L/C (letter of credit) is a promise of payment issued by the buyer's bank at the buyer's request. The seller ships the goods, presents the documents the L/C requires to the bank, and is paid. The required documents are items such as the bill of lading (the certificate that the goods were loaded on the vessel), the invoice and the packing list. The structure relies on the credit of the bank, not of the buyer's company. Some L/Cs pay as soon as documents are presented, and others pay after a set period. Since you are relying on a bank's promise, also look at whether the bank that issued the L/C is a sound one.

Between the two is documentary collection (D/P and D/A). The seller hands the shipping documents to their own bank, and the buyer's bank releases them against payment (D/P) or against a promise to pay later (D/A). It is cheaper and simpler than an L/C, but the banks only pass the documents along. They do not verify them or guarantee payment. If the buyer does not pay, the seller has to find another buyer or pay freight to bring the goods back. So it suits buyers you have traded with for some time and stable markets.

For example, with 30% in advance and the 70% balance before shipment, the goods are still in your hands even if the buyer does not pay the balance. What terms do you propose in a first deal?

Start from terms that are safe for the seller and concede one step at a time. Propose full payment in advance first, move to split payment if the buyer finds that difficult, and move to an L/C when the amount grows. If you insist on advance payment only, you can lose the order to a competitor offering better terms, so decide in advance how far you will concede. If the buyer insists on open account, look into export credit insurance. This is insurance that compensates part of the loss when you are not paid because of the buyer's bankruptcy, late payment, or conditions in the importing country such as war. It covers the loss only up to a set percentage, not in full. Damage in transit is not covered, so it is taken out separately from cargo insurance. You cannot sign up after a problem has occurred, so look into it before you give credit. Eligibility and coverage vary with the buyer's credit and country.

Payment terms are your cash plan. At the quoting stage, work out whether the advance covers the money for raw materials and how many weeks you have to hold out until the balance comes in. The timing of the balance also changes with who is responsible for which leg of transport, so set it together with your Incoterms. What do you check first when you receive an L/C?

  1. Check the dates. Confirm that the latest shipment date, the deadline for presenting documents and the L/C expiry date fit your production schedule.
  2. Check the required documents. Look for any document you cannot obtain on your own. An inspection certificate that needs the buyer's signature is one example.
  3. Compare with the contract. Check that the product description, quantity, unit price and trade terms match to the letter.
  4. Check partial shipment and transshipment. Transshipment means moving the cargo to another vessel at a port on the way. If it is prohibited and you ship that way, the documents will not match the conditions.
  5. Fix anything that does not fit before shipment. Ask the buyer to amend the L/C.

The bank compares the documents with the L/C, not with the contract. Even a single spelling difference can be raised as an issue. Document preparation is easy to get wrong, so having someone experienced handle it avoids payment delays and extra fees. When you receive an L/C, show it first to the export desk at your bank. Better still, consult the bank before the buyer applies for the L/C. Details such as fees and each bank's procedures can change, so check your bank's latest guidance before you proceed. Common mistakes with payment terms

  • Accepting payment afterward too easily because you want the order. Whether you can collect comes before the size of the first order.
  • Writing the balance timing only as "after shipment". State it as a date: within how many days after sending copies of the shipping documents, or after arrival.
  • Not deciding who pays the transfer fees. Fees taken by intermediary banks can mean less arrives than was promised.
  • Reading the L/C only after production is finished. By then there is no time to fix it.
  • Trusting an email that says the bank account has changed. Reconfirm by phone any notice of an account change that arrives just before payment. Emails impersonating your account are sometimes sent to buyers too, so agree in advance on how account details will be sent.
